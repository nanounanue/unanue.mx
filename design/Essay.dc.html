LLMs Are Variety Preservers

November 2026

On compression

Machine learning, as practiced, is the art of throwing information away. A classifier maps the world’s states onto a handful of labels; a risk score collapses a life onto a scalar. This is not a defect — compression is what makes action possible — but it has a price, and the price has a name. Cybernetics gave it one seventy years ago.1

Ashby’s law, stated plainly

A regulator holds outcomes steady only if it can match, move for move, the variety of the disturbances that reach it. Written as capacities:

V(O) ≥ V(D) − V(R)

Whatever variety the regulator cannot absorb passes through to the outcomes. No cleverness gets around the inequality; there is only moving capacity from one side to the other.

What language models keep

A conventional model fixes its output space before it sees the world: the labels are chosen, the score is bounded, the variety budget is spent at design time. A language model does something structurally different — its output space is the language itself, the highest-variety channel humans operate.2 It can answer with a distinction nobody asked it to make. That is what it means to preserve variety: the map keeps degrees of freedom the territory may yet need.

The political reading

Here the argument turns. When a model compresses, someone chose what survives the compression — and that choice is an objective function wearing a technical costume. Objective functions encode political choices. A system that preserves variety keeps those choices open a little longer; a system that destroys it forecloses them silently. Which of the two you deploy is not an engineering detail.

1. W. Ross Ashby, An Introduction to Cybernetics (1956), ch. 11: the law of requisite variety.
2. The claim concerns the class of maps a model can express after training, not its parameter count.
